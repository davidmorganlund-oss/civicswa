How local government works in Washington

Most of the decisions you feel day to day, like zoning, roads, police, parks and schools, are made close to home. Here is who makes them.

Three kinds of local government

Where you live, you are likely served by at least three local governments at once: a county, a city or town (unless you live in an unincorporated area), and several special purpose districts.

Counties

Washington has 39 counties. Counties run the courts, the jail, elections, property assessment and public health, and they handle roads, land use and policing outside city limits.

  • Most counties (32 of them) are run by a board of elected county commissioners, usually three.
  • Seven counties have their own charter: Clallam, Clark, King, Pierce, San Juan, Snohomish and Whatcom. Several of those have an elected county executive and a county council.
  • Other elected county officials include the assessor, auditor (who usually runs elections), prosecuting attorney, sheriff, treasurer and clerk.

Cities and towns

Washington has 281 cities and towns. They handle local streets, zoning and building permits, city police, parks, and often water and sewer.

  • Mayor-council cities elect a mayor who runs the city day to day, and a council that passes laws and the budget.
  • Council-manager cities elect a council that hires a professional city manager. The mayor is usually a council member chosen to chair meetings.

Special purpose districts

Special purpose districts each do one job and most have their own elected board. You may live in a school district, a port district, a public utility district, a fire district, a library district, a transit authority, a hospital district and a water or sewer district, all at once.

Their boards make real decisions about taxes, rates and services, and their elections often get little attention. Check your ballot or property tax statement to see which ones you live in.

How to take part

  • Go to a meeting. Councils, commissions and district boards meet in public. The calendar lists meetings near you.
  • Comment. Each governing body must take public comment before final action at its regular meetings. See how to speak at a local public meeting.
  • Serve. Cities and counties appoint residents to advisory boards and commissions, like planning, parks or human services. Openings are usually posted on the city or county website.
  • Vote in local elections. In Washington, most city, county, school and district offices are elected in odd-numbered years, when turnout is lowest.
