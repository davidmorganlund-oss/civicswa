What you can do in your neighborhood

You don't need a title to change things on your block. Here are practical ways to get problems fixed and get people organized.

Get a problem fixed

  • Report it to the right place. Potholes, broken streetlights, illegal dumping and graffiti usually go to the city's public works department, or to the county if you live outside city limits. Many cities have an online or app-based service request system.
  • Keep the reference number. If nothing happens, follow up with it. If nothing happens after that, contact your council member or county commissioner's office.
  • Bring neighbors. Five households reporting the same problem carries more weight than one.

Find out what's planned

  • Watch for land use notices. Big projects usually require public notice, often a sign on the property and a mailed notice to neighbors, with a comment period.
  • Ask for records. Washington's Public Records Act lets anyone request public records from a state or local agency. The agency must respond within five business days, though the response may be an estimate of when you'll get the records.
  • Read the capital plan. Cities and counties publish plans for roads, parks and facilities years in advance. Getting a project into the plan is easier than adding it later.

Get organized

  • Join or start a neighborhood association. Many cities formally recognize neighborhood associations and meet with them.
  • Hold a block party or cleanup. Cities often have simple permits for closing a residential street, and some lend cleanup supplies.
  • Make a phone tree or group chat. It sounds small, but it is what lets a neighborhood show up quickly when a decision is coming.

Take it to a meeting

When a problem needs a decision, not just a repair, bring it to your city council or county commission during public comment. See how to speak at a local public meeting and turning a personal problem into a policy ask.

  • Chapter 42.30 RCW, Open Public Meetings Act
