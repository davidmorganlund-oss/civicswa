Running for office in Washington

Thousands of local and state offices are on the ballot in Washington, and many races draw only one candidate. Here is how to get started.

Which offices are up, and when

  • Odd-numbered years: most local offices, including city councils, mayors, school boards, port commissions and other special districts.
  • Even-numbered years: the Legislature, statewide offices, most county offices, many judges and Congress.
  • Who can run: for most offices, you must be a registered voter in the area the office serves.

Filing for office

  • Filing week is one week in May. The Secretary of State and county elections offices announce exact dates each year.
  • Where you file: most candidates file with their county elections office, usually online. Statewide, legislative and congressional candidates can file with the Secretary of State.
  • The filing fee for a paid office is 1 percent of its annual salary. If you can't pay, you can file a petition with signatures from registered voters instead.
  • Primary and general: Washington uses a top-two primary in August, and the top two candidates advance to November regardless of party.

Campaign finance rules

You become a candidate as soon as you raise or spend money, reserve ad space, publicly announce, or file to run. Within two weeks of that, most candidates must register with the Public Disclosure Commission.

  • Mini reporting is for small campaigns: you raise and spend no more than $7,000, and no one but you gives more than $500.
  • Full reporting is required above that, with regular reports of every contribution and expense.
  • Very small districts (fewer than 2,000 registered voters) may not need to file with the PDC at all if the campaign stays under $7,000.

Before you file

  • Learn the job. Attend meetings of the body you want to join and read its budget.
  • Talk to people who hold or held the office about the time it takes.
  • Check your employer's rules. Some public and private jobs limit political activity.
