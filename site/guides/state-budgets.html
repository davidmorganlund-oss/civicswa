Washington's three state budgets and how to influence each

Washington writes three budgets, not one. Knowing which budget pays for what, and when decisions are made, tells you where to push.

The three budgets

  • The operating budget pays for the day-to-day work of state government: K-12 schools, colleges, health care, social services, prisons, natural resources and state agencies. It is by far the largest.
  • The capital budget pays for buildings and land: state facilities, school construction grants, parks, and grants and loans for local projects like community centers and housing.
  • The transportation budget pays for highways and bridges, ferries, the State Patrol, driver licensing, and grants for public transportation.

The calendar

  • Two-year budgets. Washington budgets cover two years, starting July 1 of odd-numbered years.
  • Supplemental budgets. In even-numbered years, the Legislature adjusts all three budgets with supplemental budgets.
  • Summer and fall: state agencies send their budget requests to the Governor.
  • December: the Governor proposes budgets.
  • January to spring: the Legislature writes its own versions, passes final budgets, and the Governor signs them, sometimes vetoing parts.

Where you can weigh in

  • Agency requests (summer and fall). If your issue depends on a state program, ask the agency or a group that works with it what it plans to request. Ideas that start in an agency request are more likely to make the Governor's budget.
  • Committee hearings (session). The House and Senate budget committees hold public hearings on each budget proposal soon after it is released. You can testify, sign in to note your position, or send written testimony. See how to weigh in on a state bill.
  • Your legislators (fall and session). Legislators request funding for projects in their districts, especially in the capital budget. Local groups and governments ask their legislators to sponsor these requests, usually before session starts.
  • Budget-related bills. Many policy bills cost money. A bill's fiscal note estimates the cost, and bills that cost money often have to clear a budget committee too.

Following the money

The state's fiscal information site shows each proposal side by side, with maps of capital and transportation projects, so you can see what changed between versions.
