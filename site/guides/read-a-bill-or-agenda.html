How to read a bill or an agenda

Bills and agendas are written in their own shorthand. Once you know a few conventions, you can tell quickly what's changing and when to show up.

Reading a Washington bill

  • The number tells you where it started. HB is a House bill, SB a Senate bill.
  • Prefixes show it has changed. "S" (substitute, as in SHB 1234) means a committee rewrote it. "2S" means a second substitute. "E" (engrossed) means it was amended on the floor.
  • Underlined text is new; struck-through text is being removed from existing law. A section marked "NEW SECTION" is entirely new.
  • Start with the bill report, a plain-language summary written by nonpartisan staff, before you read the bill itself.
  • Check the fiscal note to see the estimated cost to state and local government.
  • Look at the bill's history to see which committee has it and whether a hearing is scheduled.

All of this is on the Legislature's bill information page. Search by bill number or keyword.

Reading a local meeting agenda

  • The consent agenda groups routine items that pass in one vote without discussion, like approving minutes, small contracts and bills. Anything controversial can still end up there, so read it.
  • Public hearings are where the body takes comment on a specific item, like a zoning change or the budget.
  • Action items are votes. Discussion items or study sessions are for talking it through, often without a vote.
  • Executive session is a closed portion allowed by law for limited topics, like lawsuits or real estate negotiations. Final votes must be taken in public.
  • The packet (or staff report) has the details: the proposal, the cost and staff's recommendation. It's usually posted a few days before the meeting.

Tips

  • Agendas for regular meetings are generally posted online at least 24 hours ahead.
  • Search the packet for your street, neighborhood or issue.
  • If something is unclear, call or email the city or county clerk. Answering questions about agendas is part of their job.
