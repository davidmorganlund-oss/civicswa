How the Washington Legislature works

The Legislature can feel like a maze of numbers, cutoffs and committees. It runs on a handful of rules, and once you know them you can follow almost any bill.

Who's in it

  • 49 legislative districts. Each elects one senator, for a four-year term, and two representatives, for two-year terms.
  • The House has 98 members and is led by the Speaker.
  • The Senate has 49 members. The Lieutenant Governor presides, and the majority leader runs the Senate's business.
  • Caucuses. Each chamber's Democrats and Republicans meet separately, often in private, to decide their positions. The majority party chooses the committee chairs, and chairs decide which bills get a hearing.

The calendar

  • Sessions start the second Monday in January. In odd-numbered years the session lasts up to 105 days and writes the two-year budgets. In even-numbered years it lasts up to 60 days and adjusts them.
  • Each two-year cycle is one "biennium." A bill that doesn't pass in the first year can still pass in the second.
  • Fall and early winter are the interim. Committees hold work sessions on "committee assembly days" to preview issues. These are listed on the CivicsWA calendar.
  • Bills can be prefiled starting in December, before session begins.
  • Special sessions of up to 30 days can be called by the Governor or by a two-thirds vote of the Legislature.

How a bill becomes law

  1. Introduced. A legislator files the bill. House bills are numbered from 1000 and Senate bills from 5000.
  2. Referred to a policy committee. The chair decides whether it gets a public hearing. Most bills never do.
  3. Public hearing. Anyone can sign in with a position, send written testimony or testify. See how to weigh in on a state bill.
  4. Executive session. The committee votes, often on an amended version called a substitute.
  5. Fiscal committee, if it costs money. Bills with a cost usually go to a budget committee next.
  6. Rules Committee. This committee decides which bills reach the floor. Many bills stop here without a vote.
  7. Floor vote. The full chamber debates, can amend, and votes.
  8. The other chamber. The bill starts over: committee, hearing, Rules, floor.
  9. Agreement. If the second chamber changed the bill, the first must accept the changes ("concur") or the two sides negotiate, sometimes in a conference committee.
  10. The Governor. The Governor can sign it, let it become law without a signature, or veto it, in whole or, for most bills, in part.
  11. Effective date. Most new laws take effect 90 days after the session ends, unless the bill sets another date or has an emergency clause.

Cutoffs: why bills "die"

Each session sets cutoff dates. After each one, bills that haven't reached the next step are considered dead for the year, unless they're tied to the budget or the Legislature votes otherwise.

  • Policy committee cutoff: bills must pass their first committee.
  • Fiscal committee cutoff: bills with a cost must clear a budget committee.
  • House of origin cutoff: bills must pass the chamber where they started.
  • Opposite house cutoffs: the same steps, in the other chamber.

"Dead" isn't always final. In the first year of a biennium, bills carry over to the second year. Ideas from dead bills also come back as amendments or in the budget.

Reading the signs

  • HB, SB: House bill, Senate bill. HJM, SJM are memorials, messages to Congress or the President. Resolutions express a position or set rules.
  • S, 2S, E: a committee substitute, a second substitute, and engrossed (amended on the floor). "ESSB" means engrossed substitute Senate bill.
  • Fiscal note: the official estimate of what a bill would cost.
  • Bill report: a plain-language summary by nonpartisan staff, updated at each step.
  • Sine die: the last day of session.

How to follow what's happening

  • Look up any bill on the Legislature's bill information page, by number or keyword, to see its status, reports and hearings.
  • Watch floor sessions and many hearings live on TVW.
  • Sign up for email updates from the Legislature on bills and committees you care about.
  • Call the Legislative Hotline at 1-800-562-6000 to leave a message for your legislators.
  • Check the State Legislature listings on the CivicsWA calendar for upcoming committee meetings and what's on each agenda.

Where your voice counts most

  • Before session, when legislators decide which bills to sponsor and what to put in budget requests.
  • At the committee hearing, the main point where the public is on the record.
  • Before Rules and floor votes, with a short message to your own legislators.

Your own legislators care most about hearing from people in their district. A short message with your address carries more weight than a long one from outside.
