Your school board

Your local school board decides how your district spends its money, who runs it and which policies every school follows. You can speak at its meetings, and anyone who lives in the district and is registered to vote can run for it.

What the board decides

  • The superintendent. The board hires, supervises and evaluates the superintendent, who runs the district day to day.
  • The budget. The board adopts the district's budget every year, after a public hearing in the summer.
  • Policies. Board policies cover things like discipline, grading, attendance, safety, and how parents and students can raise complaints.
  • Goals and big decisions. The district's vision and goals, school boundaries, opening or closing a school, labor contracts, and textbooks and curriculum.
  • Levies and bonds. The board decides whether to ask voters for a levy or bond, how much, and for how long. How levies and bonds pass.

Who sits on it

  • Directors are elected in nonpartisan races in odd-numbered years. Party labels don't appear on the ballot.
  • Most boards have five directors. A few large districts have more. Most directors serve four-year terms.
  • Some districts elect directors by area (director districts), some at large, and some use a mix. Your district's website or county elections office will tell you which seat covers your address.
  • Many boards include student representatives who take part in meetings.

Start in the right place

For a problem with one student or one school, the board usually isn't the first stop. Start with the teacher, then the principal, then the district office. Most districts have a complaint policy that spells out the steps.

Go to the board when the issue is a policy, the budget, or something that affects many students, or when the district's own process hasn't worked.

Speak at a meeting

  • You have a right to comment. Under state law, boards must take public comment at or before every regular meeting where they take final action. They can set time limits and sign-up rules.
  • You can comment in writing. If the board accepts written comment, it has to share it with the board members. Watch for a deadline before the meeting.
  • Can't attend in person? If a disability, limited mobility or another barrier makes it hard to come, the board must let you comment remotely when that's feasible.
  • Sign up early. Many districts open sign-up a few days ahead and cap the number of speakers. Check the district's board meeting page.
  • Work sessions and study sessions are open to watch, but many don't take public comment. Regular meetings are where you speak and where votes happen.

New to public comment? How to speak at a public meeting and your two-minute pitch walk through it.

Read the agenda first

Boards post agendas before each meeting, usually on the district website or a board portal. The consent agenda bundles routine items, like hiring lists, contracts and field trips, into one vote without discussion. Any director can ask to pull an item off it for a separate vote, so if something you care about is buried there, tell the board before the meeting. How to read an agenda.

Boards can meet privately in executive session only for limited reasons, like personnel matters, real estate and lawsuits. Any final vote still has to happen in public.

Run for the board

  • Who can run: a registered voter who lives in the district, and in the director district if the seat is elected by area.
  • When: candidates file during filing week in May of odd-numbered years, online or with the county elections office.
  • What it takes: regular evening meetings, reading packets, and visits to schools. Directors are not full-time and are paid little or nothing.
