Understanding the Constitution

You live under two constitutions: the U.S. Constitution and the Washington State Constitution. Here is what each one does.

The U.S. Constitution

  • Articles I to III set up the three branches: Congress makes laws, the President carries them out, and the courts interpret them.
  • The Bill of Rights (the first 10 amendments) protects freedoms like speech, religion, the press, assembly and petition, and sets rules for searches, trials and punishment.
  • Later amendments ended slavery, guaranteed equal protection and due process (the 14th), and expanded the right to vote regardless of race, sex or age 18 and over.
  • Federalism. Powers not given to the federal government are reserved to the states or the people.

The Washington State Constitution

  • Adopted in 1889, when Washington became a state.
  • It has its own Declaration of Rights (Article I). Washington courts sometimes read it as protecting rights more broadly than the U.S. Constitution, for example on privacy.
  • It gives voters direct power. Since 1912, Article II has let voters pass laws by initiative and overturn laws by referendum. See state initiatives and referendums.
  • It sets up state government, including the Legislature, the nine statewide elected officials and the courts.
  • It makes education a priority. Article IX calls educating Washington's children the state's "paramount duty."

How each one changes

  • U.S. Constitution: usually two-thirds of both houses of Congress propose an amendment, then three-quarters of the states ratify it.
  • Washington Constitution: two-thirds of both the state House and Senate propose an amendment, then a majority of Washington voters approve it.

Read them yourself

Both are short enough to read in an afternoon. The annotated U.S. Constitution on Congress.gov explains how courts have read each part.
